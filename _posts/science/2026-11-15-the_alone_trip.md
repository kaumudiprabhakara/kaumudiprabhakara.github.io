Who among us hasn’t dreamed of getting away from it all and living a “simple” life in the wilderness? It is a reasonable fantasy, with all that’s happening in the world. Keith Stewart, at the height of the cold war, boldly realized this fantasy by living alone in one of the remotest corners of Earth - the northern territory of Australia - for about four months. The book, essentially a compilation of his diary entries, describes what follows. Setting up camp, encountering diverse wildlife, struggling with the intense heat, foraging for food, and most importantly for him, finding the time and space for thoughts. 

Remote Australia meant an uninhabited, hot and arid desert to me. To my surprise, I learnt that the northern part of the Northern territory, while certainly sparsely inhabited, has a tropical monsoon climate, with rivers, forests, and grasslands teeming with wildlife. Stewart chose a gorge in this area for his introspection in isolation.

The writing is beautifully descriptive and evocative of not only his emotions but also the wilderness. The gorge reads like a slice of paradise. About a month into his stay, Stewart discovers that his light meter is out of battery and undertakes a long hike back to the nearest town to buy new batteries so that he can photograph the gorge. Reading this in 2026, it was a shock that for one month he hadn’t even thought to take a picture! But perhaps this shouldn’t be surprising - after all, from the book it is clear that Stewart experienced profound peace living simply in the beautiful environs. He describes his life so far, which, though exciting, seems to be lacking something he needs, the views of his parents, and relationships with friends and partners. I detected more than a tinge of regret in the beginning of the book for bygone friendships, but towards the end he is ready to move on: “The past is a mist, obscuring the smiles of friends and lovers, now scattered and gone.”  He questions his actions and motivations, interprets poems and begins to realize that people, including himself, are complex. He gains insights on life from nature over the course of his camping, coming to regret some of his worse decisions. He compares humans and the wildlife around him, concluding that unlike the animals, who have “integrity” and “are without guile”, humans are “self-aggrandizing”, “selfish” and “self-destructive”. He looks for a kinder philosophy.

He encounters reptiles, amphibians, insects, flies and more. The descriptions of the diverse species of each of the organisms he meets (and the sheer diversity in the small area around his camp boggles the mind!), their behaviour and his interactions with them make for an enjoyable read. I tried and failed to pick a favorite description - the birds with colorful plumage, the beautiful snakes, the many-shaped lizards and frogs… The description of his friendship with a monitor lizard, with all its ups and downs, is enchanting. I only wish at the end of the book there was an index linking each description of an organism to its name. 

The honest writing makes it possible for the reader to re-live his experiences. The annoying flies, the heat, the sweat, the fear of sudden rains and floods, the need to ration and protect supplies, and the lack of plumbing on the one hand reveal the many challenges, and on the other hand, discovering wildlife, the beautiful gorge, the joys of swimming and gardening, and befriending animals, reveal the allure of such a life. Stewrat honestly highlights his struggles to come to terms with contradictions in his ideals and needs - the need versus the desire to eat, the instinct to hurt and kill animals you respect, and his impact on the nature around him. Some of his actions are mortifying, and Stewart’s uprightness in presenting the good and the bad, while admitting to and regretting his mistakes, is indeed praiseworthy. 

The book is light, humorous, and easy to read. For example, he says ostensibly seriously “Australians also love Pavlova and dispute New Zealand’s claim of origin. They insist they invented this iconic Southern Hemisphere dessert, but anyone born in New Zealand knows they are wrong, that Australians would not be capable of such creativity.” The writing also feels raw and unedited, albeit in a good way: it transmits the emotion at the time of writing. For example, the tenses change occasionally across sections, which to me speaks to Stewart’s state of mind when he wrote it. I found it hard to put down the book once I started reading.

I’m inspired by Stewart’s discipline and resolve. It would have been easy to give up because of the flies, or the heat or the lack of shelter. It would have been easy to slip into lethargy when there’s no job to get to at 9 in the morning, or anyone to judge you if you haven’t made your bed. But he maintains a regular schedule, including time for exercising and creative cooking (I look forward to trying his bread recipe).

At the beginning and the end, Stewart alludes to travels in Africa that sound really exciting. Surprisingly he hasn’t written books about them; rather his books have been about gardening and now this book about his solitary sojourn in remote Australia. This fact and the points he stresses about nature in the book give him away as someone who values nurturing and being at one with nature - an ideal we should all emulate. 

In this fast paced world, where travel seems to be checking items off bucket lists, this book is a breath of much needed fresh air - epitomizing slow travel. It shows there is so much to learn simply by immersing ourselves in a new place: the culture, the beauty, and the challenges of and attitudes to life. I believe this holds for any place, not necessarily a remote corner of the world, although I certainly lived vicariously through Stewart in Umbrawarra. This is the travelogue the world needs today.
